> [!QUOTE] p. 78 The Intentional Stance
> The intentional stance is the strategy of interpreting the behavior of an entity (person, animal, artifact, or whatever) by treating it *as if* it were a rational agent who governed its "choice" of "action" by a "consideration" of its "beliefs" and "desires."
^intentional-stance-definition

## Thoughts
- "How does [[Folk Psychology]] work?" [p. 77]
    - We *assume* others are not idiotic and are not self-destructive
    - In [[Game Theory]] terms, they are "rational"
- When you use folk psychology, you are using the Intentional Stance
- [[#^intentional-stance-definition]] "Intention" as in "what their intentions/goals are," not as in "deliberate"
- This stance is useful in more than just folk psychology: it can be used to analyze or explain complex problems or ideas in a simple way
- **Extra tool**: Nibbling. Take a small "bite" out of a problem instead of trying to tackle the whole thing. Figure out what the problem is about. **then** attack it from a better position
    - You don't need to fully understand a problem before you start exploring it
- Two other stances:
    - **Physical**: "The standard laborious method of the physical sciences" [p. 79]
        - Physics, chemistry, etc.
        - The Laws of the Universe
        - Predicting the "more interesting" behaviors of things (living and designed, as opposed to mundane things like stones) is not usually practical with this stance
    - **Design**: Analyzing something by its design
        - "It's an alarm clock, so I *should* be able to set an alarm somehow"
        - Requires we make come assumptions beyond when the physical stance require:
            - The object is what I think it is (or designed to be what I think it is designed to be)
            - The object is not broken/malfunctioning
                - Including not poorly designed to the point where it's nonfunctional
        - These assumptions make it riskier than the physical stance in that it is more easily possible to be wrong.
        - However, these assumptions also make it easier to use than the physical stance: I don't need to know how an engine works in order to drive a car
- The Intentional Stance is riskier than the other two: it requires more assumptions
- However, it's also more useful when understanding more complex things
    - "What does this thing *want* to do?"
    - This won't always lead to perfect predictability though. We often need to fall back to the other stages for that.
- There are two arguments for how the intentional stance works: [p. 83]
    1. We learn thousands of "Laws:"
        - People can see what's in front of them
        - People will work together towards a common goal
        - Stubbed toes hurt
        - Etc.
    2. We have "rough cast" laws generated on-demand using our intuition on what a rational response would be
- LLMs **require** the first to operate
    - They have no or very poor intuition
- Humans probably learn a mixture: societal rules and natural intuition
- It is important to remember the agent's perspective, needs, and environment in this stance
- An agent can be almost anything: even a thermostat
- The intentional stance gives you the "spec" of the system [p. 84]
    - This might be a really interesting way to software engineer:
        1. Start in the intentional stance. Describe the system. What does/will it do? Why? What are its *intentions*?
            - [[Gherkin feature files]]
        2. Drop to design stance to implements it
